One of the difficulties I've found as a university student is understanding what courses to take. Obviously there's resources online outlining generic information about the overall topics and maybe some previous grade distributions. However, the most useful information about specific courses is information I've heard from fellow students. The issue is, however, that all that this information remains with students when they graduate or go off on co-op. So one evening earlier this summer while I was procrastinating my undergraduate research work, I decided I'd put together a little project to try and fix this problem that was on my mind and solve the problem for the students arriving while I'm heading off. By about 4:30am that night I had an MVP, and a few nights later, I had a much better one. A few more days later I did some iterative testing with friends to optimize the experience for first-time users. I intended for the project to simply be a quick one-and-done application I could put together so I could jot down some notes about 35+ courses I've taken over the last five years with hopes it may help some students further down the road, however I started figuring out ways I could better optimize the application. From improving the proprietary auth with salted passwords to a more user-friendly magic-link design, to adding document upload support and visualizations, I kept thinking of more features to add. For the next week, I spent every night iteratively improving: more design iterations, pulling the full course catalog from the UBC course calendar, new fields to describe courses, debouncing when searching, improving the domain's SEO etc. The greatest part was that I was working on it purely because it was something I've been meaning to build for the past several years and one night, motivated by procrastination, decided to write the first commit. It seems that in an increasingly competitve climate, every productive minute of every one of my days is spent on schoolwork, my startup, internship tasks, undergraduate research, interview prep, leetcode, networking, or anything else that would inch me further towards that coveted full time offer at my dream company. Despite the project containing several technical challenges that pushed me to think in a different way or ask for third-party input, what I'm most proud of is that I worked on the project because I wanted to, not because it would help me. It reminded me to stay curious and solve the problems that are actually in front of me. I hope to bring this same curiousity to Hack The North 2026 and connect with like-minded individuals.
No AI was used in writing or editing of this response, just an honest recount of reconnecting with my passion :).
